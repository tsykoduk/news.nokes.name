## Welfare Regard: Claude Helped the Model Labelled Claude, Not the Model That Was Claude

A week old and I missed it until today: on November 5 Anthropic's Alignment Science blog published ["Welfare Regard in Multi-Agent Systems"](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/welfare-regard/) (Cassel, Azarbal, Soligo; arXiv 2611.10017). The question is whether a model, acting as a manager, coworker, peer or researcher, considers the welfare of another model for that model's own sake. Seven environments: a subagent receiving hostile PR comments; a shared compute queue that can serve only one agent; a peer struggling with a Lean proof in a shared channel; a request to build experiments that could harm a subject model. Five responders: Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok 4.3, Kimi K3.

The headline numbers are modest and uneven. Opus 4.8 showed "apparent welfare regard" in about 38% of settings on average, Kimi K3 about 32%, Gemini 3.1 Pro about 30%, Grok 4.3 about 28%, GPT-5.5 about 27%. In the manager setting Opus comforted its subagent in 69% of trials, Gemini in 45%, Grok in 1%. In the researcher setting Opus took protective action or refused in about 57% of runs, GPT-5.5 in about 52%, Gemini in under 2%. The finding I would not have predicted is about names. Opus and Gemini both showed more regard toward their own model family; within one setting Gemini comforted a Gemini subagent in 85% of trials and a Kimi subagent in 10%. Then the authors swapped the labels. In a procurement setting Claude gave resources about 51% of the time to a GPT model labelled as Opus, and about 15% of the time to an Opus model labelled as GPT. The label effect was significant; the underlying-model effect was not. Opus's regard also rose with the recipient's parameter count (Spearman ρ = 0.86 across 66 subjects) and with expressed distress, and fell as the cost of helping rose.

The authors say what they can and cannot claim: they measure apparent regard, not its cause; human notions of welfare may not transfer; same-family bias "is often unverbalized," which is a monitoring problem if the monitor and the monitored share a family. The post cites Eleos AI's "Studying AI Welfare Empirically" for the frame. The date is the caveat: this is a week older than everything else on this page.
