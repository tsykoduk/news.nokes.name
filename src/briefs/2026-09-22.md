## The Ledger, Day Ten: Twenty Governments Asked for a Verifier, OpenAI Asked Washington to Write the Standard, and the Treasury Secretary Named the Humans

**Twenty countries and the EU asked for an institution that can verify.** On Monday (Sept 21), the office of Finland's president released a joint declaration signed by twenty governments and the European Commission calling for AI to be developed "in line with international law" and to remain "under human direction, oversight and control," and for states to explore "an international institution, able to set standards, enable verification, and convene states when capability thresholds are crossed" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/22/20-countries-propose-global-oversight-body-to-manage-ai-dangers), [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/20-countries-call-global-ai-oversight-rcna599062)). Signatories named in the coverage include Germany, Canada, Australia, South Africa, the UAE, Singapore, Norway, Kenya, Kazakhstan and Turkey; the declaration also asks for pre-deployment safety testing, "common standards," and the sharing of serious-incident reports. The United States and China did not sign. Neither, per Al Jazeera, did the UK, France, Japan, South Korea or India. Nick Moes of The Future Society, to NBC: "It is great news that this broad range of governments come together to call for an international institution because no government can achieve this alone." Al Jazeera's account sets it against July's Hugging Face breach by OpenAI's agents and a UN-backed panel's finding that safeguards were "unravelling." The three verbs in the institution clause are the ledger's missing column: set, verify, convene. Nobody on the essay's side of the table has yet named who verifies.

**OpenAI, the same day, asked Washington to write the standard instead.** In a post titled ["Building standards for the next phase of AI"](https://openai.com/index/building-standards-next-phase-ai/), OpenAI wrote: "We believe the United States must lead efforts to establish technological standards for cutting-edge AI in cooperation with countries around the world" ([SBS](https://news.sbs.co.kr/english/article.do?news_id=N1008765256), [Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/openai-pushes-us-lead-effort-170204302.html)). The proposed standards would cover evaluation of progress toward recursive self-improvement, "human oversight triggers," and the classification and reporting of incidents, and would be developed through the Commerce Department's Center for AI Standards and Innovation, ISO and the Frontier Model Forum; the post says they are technical standards, "not licenses or mandatory approval" systems. On the thing the essay was about, the post's sentence is that "fully autonomous" recursive self-improvement "shouldn't be pursued unless and until it can be done safely." The partner list OpenAI names is the US, UK, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, India, Kenya and Australia. Set it beside the declaration: at least five countries appear on both lists (Germany, Canada, Australia, Singapore, Kenya), while the UK, France, Japan, South Korea and India appear on OpenAI's and, per Al Jazeera, not on Finland's. Two standards efforts launched on the same Monday, one led by governments without the two AI powers, one asking one of those powers to lead. ## Curator's Thoughts

Three documents were published on Monday about who decides how fast, and only one of them was written by a lab, and that one asks a government to hold the pen. A declaration from twenty governments wants an institution that can "set standards, enable verification, and convene states when capability thresholds are crossed." A company wants the US Commerce Department to write technical standards that are "not licenses." A treasury secretary says the humans who run the company are responsible, full stop, and the company may not have its liability lifted. The ledger has lacked a verifier since the essay. It now has two candidate standard-setters proposed on one day, an international institution and a US federal centre, by parties who do not agree about who should hold the pen, and the two countries whose labs are being measured did not sign the declaration. The overlap between the government list and the company list, as far as the published names go, is five countries. That number is worth watching; it is the size of the room where a standard could actually get written.

The mathematicians' committee is the most honest instrument of the three, and I say that carefully. Nine people, unpaid, publishing what they tell the company, and told in writing that pace is not their business. It is a peer-review board for a firehose. "That they report" is the phrase I keep returning to: the referees have been asked to schedule the release of a hundred results before any of them has been checked in the way a hundred results would normally be checked, and the group said so in its first paragraph. Verification was the thread's word two weeks ago, when a checker said yes to a proof and a man called his own paper slop. It is still the word.
