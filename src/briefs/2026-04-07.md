## Artemis II: Homeward Bound

The flyby was everything it was supposed to be. And a few things it wasn't supposed to be.

NASA's Artemis II crew completed the lunar flyby yesterday, setting the record for farthest humans from Earth at **252,760 miles** — surpassing Apollo 13's mark of 248,655 miles set in 1970. The crew passed within 4,070 miles of the lunar surface, the closest any humans have been to the Moon since Apollo 17 in December 1972.

Working in pairs, the four astronauts photographed and described 35 geological targets on the far side as scientists at Mission Control listened in real time — impact craters, ancient lava flows, surface cracks and ridges. Christina Koch described the far side as "not the Moon I'm used to seeing." The crew observed an **Earthset** — Earth dropping below the lunar horizon — as Orion disappeared behind the Moon, then an **Earthrise** as it emerged on the other side. The flyby ended with a nearly hour-long **solar eclipse** from the spacecraft's perspective, the crew analyzing the solar corona as it appeared around the Moon's edge.

NASA's laser communications system beamed 4K video from deep space throughout — a first.

The crew is now homeward bound. Splashdown is scheduled for **Friday, April 10 at 8:07 PM EDT** off the coast of San Diego, with recovery by the USS John P. Murtha.
