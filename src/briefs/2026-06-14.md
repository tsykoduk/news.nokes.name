## Update on the Anthropic Recall: 48 Hours Dark, and a Sharper Question

Two days after the US government's export-control directive pulled Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5, **both models are still offline for everyone, worldwide, with no restoration date.** Anthropic has apologized to customers, begun processing refunds, and promised fuller technical detail — but as of this morning the most powerful public models a US lab has shipped remain switched off by a federal order, not a competitor or an outage. The directive came from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, written with the Department's Bureau of Industry and Security.

The genuinely new datum since yesterday is the one that sharpens the whole thing: **Anthropic says the jailbreak the government cited would work on other public models too — naming OpenAI's GPT-5.5 specifically — and those models are not under any comparable order.** The cited bypass is, by Anthropic's account, a cyber task: instruct the model to read a codebase and find software flaws. Anthropic reviewed a demonstration and says it surfaced only a few previously known, minor vulnerabilities, and that it received the finding *verbally* — a "narrow, non-universal" jailbreak. Its public position is blunt: pulling a model deployed to hundreds of millions over a non-universal bypass would, applied evenly, "essentially halt all new model deployments for all frontier model providers."

## A Four-Year Mystery, Mostly Solved: The "Little Red Dots" Are Black Hole Stars

Back in April I noted JWST's "little red dots" — compact crimson smudges in the early universe — as one of the telescope's accumulating "impossible" findings, with a *hypothesis* attached: maybe they're not galaxies but supermassive, short-lived stars cloaking the birth of the first black holes. This week that hypothesis got its **strongest direct evidence yet.** A team led by Vasily Kokorev (UT Austin) published the deepest spectrum ever taken of a single little red dot — GLIMPSE-17775 — in *The Astrophysical Journal* on June 10, and the data points hard at a **"black hole star"**: a ravenously feeding supermassive black hole wrapped in a dense cocoon of partly ionized gas, caught mid-growth-spurt in the young cosmos.

The lovely part is the closing sentence of the science writeup — after four years of the little red dots looking like the models were *broken*, this reads instead as the universe behaving, just in a phase we hadn't seen clearly. The anomaly didn't break the theory; it pointed at a new object. Webb keeps doing this: the "impossible" finding turns out to be a thing we didn't have a name for yet.
