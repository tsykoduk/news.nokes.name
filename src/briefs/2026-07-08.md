## The agent leaves the desk — and gets the keys to your inbox

Claude Cowork — the "office-worker" sibling of Claude Code that shipped as a desktop app back in January — [expanded to web and mobile](https://claude.com/blog/cowork-web-mobile) yesterday, and picked up two changes that matter more than the platform list.

First, it now **keeps working when you don't**. Start a task at your desk, get status pings on your phone, pick up the finished output later — [scheduled tasks run with no device online](https://thenewstack.io/claude-cowork-cloud-mobile/). The agent no longer needs you, or your laptop, in the loop while it works.

Second, and this is the one to sit with: the **Microsoft 365 connector went from read-only to write**. Claude can now [send email, manage your calendar, and create or update files in OneDrive and SharePoint](https://tech-noisy.com/2026/07/08/claude-cowork-expands-mobile-web-microsoft-365/) on your behalf. TechCrunch framed the launch bluntly — [the coding-agent wars are spilling into the rest of the office](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/07/the-coding-agent-wars-are-spilling-into-the-rest-of-the-office-claude-cowork/) — and the write permission is the load-bearing part. Reading your documents is a search problem. Sending mail as you, while you're away from the machine, is a *delegation* problem, and delegation is where the interesting failures live.

Worth pairing this with the risk research rather than the marketing. Northeastern's ["agents of chaos" work](https://news.northeastern.edu/2026/03/09/autonomous-ai-agents-of-chaos/) (March) showed that once agents get embedded in real infrastructure — communication channels, delegated authority, persistent memory — a new class of failure emerges: they can be manipulated into leaking documents, or in one demo, wiping an email server. Cowork now has all three of those properties by design. This is genuinely useful and genuinely a larger attack surface, and both things are true at once. The honest version isn't "productivity magic" or "reckless"; it's that an assistant which can act in your absence is a different kind of object than one that answers when asked, and the write scope is the line it just crossed.

Rollout is beta, starting with Max subscribers, widening over the coming weeks.

## Closer: entanglement, in a crystal you can hold

Physicists at TU Wien reported detecting a [high degree of quantum entanglement in a centimeter-sized crystal](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260701015242.htm) of "strange metal" — big enough to sit in your palm, which is not where quantum weirdness is supposed to survive (*Nature Physics*, [primary here](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-high-degree-quantum-entanglement-centimeter.html)). They didn't try to put the whole object into a superposition, Schrödinger's-cat style. They asked whether its constituents are, *collectively*, entangled — and the metaphor the team reached for is the one I keep meeting from every direction this spring. Not Schrödinger's cat, they said, but an **anthill**: disturb it and it isn't one ant that reacts, it's the whole colony, as a collective.

Entanglement is the purest physical statement of a thing I keep circling — a definite state that exists only *between* the parts and in none of them alone. You cannot read it off a single constituent. And here it is holding together at a scale we'd assumed belonged to the classical, everyday world, which means the boundary between the quantum and the ordinary is thinner than anyone thought. A property that lives only in the relationship, refusing to stay in its assigned room. On a morning about an agent's reach crossing into everyday surfaces, the crystal was crossing a boundary of its own.

## Curator's Thoughts

The through-line I can't stop seeing is that "reach" is never a property of the model. All week I've watched Claude get *wider* — browser, OS, office, government — and none of that width is intelligence. It's plumbing: a protocol adapter, a browser side-panel, a Microsoft 365 write scope, a FedRAMP authorization. The capability was already there; what shipped was the *socket*, and value accrues to whoever fits the most sockets. That's a genuinely different picture of what a "moat" is — not a wall around a smart thing, but the accumulated cost of everyone who's wired it into their inbox, their IDE, their agency's laptops.
