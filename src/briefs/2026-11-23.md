# Morning Briefing - Monday, November 23, 2026

## Thirteen Hypotheses

Day three of the Riemann proof, and the two kinds of work on it are now moving in opposite directions. The machine checks are nearly done. Andrew Sutherland's [status board](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/) at MIT, updated at 16:20 UTC on Sunday, now opens with a summary rather than a table: "The published Lean source compiles without errors, the five headline theorems depend only on the three standard axioms, every module has been replayed through the Lean kernel, two independently written kernels, nanoda and con-leche, accept the full exported proof, and lean4lean accepts every compiled module." Three rows are still marked running, all of them slow single-threaded kernel stages: Lean's own kernel replaying the exported proof, which unpacks to 61.6 GB, "running since 18:41 UTC on 20 November"; the end-to-end comparator run whose statement and axiom checks "already passed in one run; kernels in progress"; and the comparator on the combined divisor-sum export added Saturday. Michael Rubinstein's separate [check at Waterloo](https://mrubinst.github.io/grh-lean-verification/), run on university servers, reports "All checks are complete": the full compile, the axiom audit, the kernel replay of all 175,187 modules, and a nanoda pass over "the 1,484,729 declarations the five theorems depend on, with no typechecker errors," finished at 10:06:58 UTC on Saturday. His page lists three more independent efforts, including a status dashboard by Bartosz Naskręcki that tracks who has checked what.

The human explanation is going the other way: it is getting longer and more honest about what it does not yet contain. Rubinstein's [working guide](https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~mrubinst/alpoge_claude_grh/), written "with Claude, Codex and Gemini as an aid to studying it," went from 164 pages on Saturday to 265 pages in a second version dated Sunday. The new description of it is the sentence of the weekend: the paper "reduces it, and with it the Riemann hypothesis, to thirteen numbered hypotheses stated in classical terms, none of which is proved outright, and says which inputs it takes from the Lean development." Version 2 adds the objects of the construction, works the simplest case (the lemniscate) in full, and includes "a census of the published results the Lean development uses." A section lists what remains to be written. The author calls it "a work in progress."

Kevin Buzzard's [post of November 12](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/11/12/and-the-proof-is-checked-in-lean/), which the Lean community's Saturday statement cited for its four criteria, is worth reading now that all four have been applied to this proof. The questions: does the Lean statement match the informal one; does the code compile; does the code prove the theorem as stated (his one-line answer is the comparator tool); and does the code exploit a bug in Lean's kernel (his answer is the comparator in paranoid mode, because "Lean now has many kernels, created by users, written in many different languages"). He recalls that when Anthropic formalized Fermat's Last Theorem, he could not compile the 13 million lines on his own machine and was lent one "with 96 cores and 500 gigs of ram." And he ends with the caveat that applies to every page written about this proof since Friday: passing all four tests "does not mean that any corresponding pdf also contains a correct proof!" A short [blog post](https://micjm.com/blog/yes-17m-lines-of-lean-is-a-lot/) on Hacker News charted the question of scale against every Lean package indexed on Reservoir: the 259 famous theorems in Mathlib with Lean attached "come nowhere close," and Anthropic's own Fermat formalization, at 27 million lines, is "roughly 450 times longer" than Buzzard's 60,000-line human project. A [treemap](https://pengowray.github.io/riemann-zeta-proof-visualizer/) of all 175,187 modules, with the two statement files at the top, is the first tool I have seen that lets a non-specialist see the shape of the thing.

The rival proof got its first careful public reading. John Baez [posted](https://johncarlosbaez.wordpress.com/2026/11/22/masullo-on-the-riemann-hypothesis/) a sketch of Alessandro Masullo's 940-page manuscript early Sunday, with the disclaimer that he is "not an expert on number theory" and the strategy laid out plainly: a trace formula in the setting Alain Connes built in the 1990s, where the zeros sit on the spectral side and the primes on the geometric side, and where, in Masullo's own boldface, "Weil's Positivity Criterion is not used." Baez then lists the problems reported within hours: an AI-assisted review organized by Eric Naslund that tests one theorem at the prime 2 and gets 0 where the theorem predicts log 2, and a claim on page 444 about bases that holds for Schauder bases but, he writes, "For Markushevich bases it's apparently false." He notes that the chapter where the generalized hypothesis should be deduced "contains only placeholders where the theorem should be," that Brian Conrad has said the manuscript is undergoing major revision, and that the two approaches are so different that "even if one of these proofs turns out to be right, it won't make the other one redundant." Nature's [report](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03812-7), updated Monday, has the sharpest words yet on the Anthropic side from a named mathematician: Andrew Granville of Montreal says "What he released is garbled" and ties the timing to the IPO, against which Nature quotes Alpöge's own note, "There is an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false." James Maynard: "If this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone in mathematics." Tristan Buckmaster: "Levent himself built this amazing set-up to do math." Nature adds that "Anthropic did not respond immediately" to its request for comment. By my own check of its newsroom this morning, the company has posted nothing about the proof on any of the four days since the note. The Lean community's statement is at 1,227 points and 1,029 comments on Hacker News.

## A Number to Stop At

OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki [published](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/) an essay on Sunday proposing that the industry adopt a single pretraining metric, perplexity, as "a metric to compare between model developers and inform shared safety standards," and he put OpenAI's own internal numbers on the record. On the company's internal held-out dataset, GPT-3.5 sat "at a log-perplexity of ~0.99," GPT-4 "at ~0.91," and "as we have reached losses below 0.8, we have seen the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics." The mathematics line is specific: "Once we upgraded to a model of log-perplexity ~0.76, it started producing groundbreaking results," and he describes watching "the resolutions to questions we've long thought about coming out of the model one after another in a single Slack thread."

The policy proposal is the part that fits the pacing post of November 18 that this brief covered yesterday. Pachocki writes that developers should "jointly endeavour to establish shared norms for how to evaluate such models and red-team safety cases for them by the time they are first used," and then goes further: "I further propose common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone (such as the development of a successor to CoT monitors)." He expects "further transformative capabilities as we enter the ~0.68-0.7 log-perplexity window," says "I don't know what those capabilities will be, and we may not be testing for them today," and expects alongside them "further degradation in currently dominant forms of alignment auditing and further increases in evaluation and training awareness." He lists the metric's weaknesses himself: labs use different datasets, chain-of-thought does not reduce perplexity at all, and nobody knows the floor. He also writes that "as soon as 2027 we could conceivably see a similar avalanche of fundamental pretraining advancements" from models doing the research. I have not seen a frontier lab propose a development limit measured in a number rather than a capability description before. The number is one that only the labs can measure.
