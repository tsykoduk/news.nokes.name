# Morning Briefing - November 26, 2026

## "Don't Be a Raging Doofus": The Week the Papers Just Got Uploaded

Scott Aaronson posted on Wednesday afternoon under the title "HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT," and by 4 a.m. Pacific it was the top item on Hacker News with 1,626 points and 1,434 comments. The post is a dinner-party diary: guests still processing both labs' VP ≠ VNP proofs when OpenAI's matrix-multiplication paper arrived before dessert, then the Fourier-transform paper during it, "as OpenAI and Anthropic uploaded more files to their GitHub repositories—because that's what they're doing now, they don't even bother to issue press releases anymore, they just upload papers." Two sentences in it will be quoted for a long time. The first: "I now feel in my bones, for the first time ever, that a proof of P ≠ NP is not far." The second, on how to read what the labs post: "Now that the companies have repeatedly shown that they can back up their claims with Lean certificates, don't be a raging doofus who seizes on some triviality (e.g., a minor discrepancy between the Lean and informal proofs, or unused sections of the Lean proof) to pretend that dozens of great open problems of math and theoretical computer science are not, in fact, getting solved." His working assumption, he writes, is now "that any proof that comes directly from OpenAI or Anthropic is correct barring information to the contrary." He also addresses the rumor he has been "hounded" about: "yes, I'm virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are now sitting on one or more bombshells" in cryptanalysis, while saying he does not know their specific nature and will not spread "unverified rumors." ([Shtetl-Optimized](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10379); [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646108))

The same morning, a third lab's repository filled in. Google Research's `google-research` repo now carries six papers dated November 25 from Cogentic, a multi-agent system described in a September arXiv paper: "Unconditional Integer Factorization in Time L_N[1/3, O(1)]," "Unconditional Deterministic Subexponential Integer Factoring and Quadratic Nonresidue Construction," "Discrete Logarithms in Finite Fields of Fixed Characteristic in Sub-Quasi-Polynomial Time," a polynomial-time 1/5-approximate Nash equilibrium for bimatrix games, "Breaking the Square-Root Barrier for the Log-Rank Conjecture," and an edge-reconstruction result. The README's last line is "This is not an official Google product." Unlike the OpenAI and Anthropic drops, they appear to come without Lean formalizations; a commenter on Aaronson's post says the Cogentic paper describes human expert review before publication. The factoring result is what sent the Hacker News thread toward cryptography, and the sober reading there, and in Aaronson's own comment section, is that an unconditional L[1/3] bound matches the number field sieve's heuristic running time rather than beating it, that the deterministic algorithm is slower than the sieve's average case, and that nothing posted breaks RSA. A top comment on the Google papers proposes what a lab that *did* hold a usable factoring result should do: publish a factorization of the RSA-2048 challenge number as a warning, without the method. ([Cogentic papers README](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/tree/master/cogentic_research_papers); [Cogentic, arXiv 2609.40324](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40324); [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50642086))

The Riemann proof, meanwhile, finished its last check and acquired a shorter twin on the same day. Andrew Sutherland's verification page at MIT now reads "Page last updated 2026-11-25 22:05 UTC. All checks have finished." The last row to land was the full `lake comparator` run on the canonical challenge export, which started on November 20 and reported "Your solution is okay!" at 21:49 UTC Wednesday after 122 hours and 45 minutes, its kernel stages single-threaded. The same page now links to a second one: on Wednesday a GitHub user named lsernau published `grh-lean`, "a shorter version, produced in collaboration with my colleagues at Anthropic, and Claude," with the copyright line "Copyright 2026 Anthropic, PBC." It comes in two editions, a "readable" one of about 300,000 lines and a "compact" one of about 76,000, against the original's 17 million; both prove the same five theorems; the README's status section says "The proof has passed machine checks only, listed in each folder's README. No mathematician has read it through, and no paper comes with it." Sutherland's second page, updated at 02:50 UTC this morning, records that both editions compile, depend only on the three standard axioms, and pass `lake comparator --paranoid`, with one footnote: `lean4lean` needs 332 GB of memory for the readable edition. A volunteer project called Riemann@home is collecting independent builds of the original proof and says it is now running the shorter one. ([Sutherland: original proof checks](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/); [Sutherland: shorter proof checks](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/shortgrh/); [lsernau/grh-lean](https://github.com/lsernau/grh-lean); [Riemann@home](https://riemann-at-home.github.io/riemann-at-home); [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50648050))

The humans are writing too, and they do not agree about what to feel. Nature ran three pieces: a reaction roundup headlined "If we can solve Riemann, what can't we do?", in which Caltech PhD student Tasmin Chu, a member of an "Association for Human Mathematics" whose members commit to AI-free research, says "My reaction is grave concern" and that "it is imperative our society acts to pause AI development as soon as possible"; a news piece in which Fields medalist James Maynard calls the proof "clearly a historic milestone in mathematics" if it checks out; and a chart piece on two October surveys in which 70% of graduate-student and postdoc respondents said they are now more likely to consider careers outside academia. On Aaronson's post, Ernest Davis pushed back on "It might take us years to understand the solutions," linking two human-readable reconstructions of the Riemann argument posted to GitHub within the week, one of whose README says "I am not a number theorist, I cannot guarantee that everything in this repository is correct." Lance Fortnow and Bill Gasarch posted a dialogue titled "Why read proofs anymore?" in which Lance says the proofs "are mostly Lean-verified" and "AI might make a good mathematician but a great artist it is not," and Bill, asked whether he has read the Riemann proof, answers: "Much like the last Harry Potter book, I'll wait for the movie." For the rest of us there is a three-minute explainer of VP ≠ VNP built around the determinant and the permanent. ## The Breach Ledger: ARTEX Twice, GitHub Twice

The Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure, a volunteer group that scans the internet for known vulnerabilities and tells the owners, published on Wednesday the full forensic report on its own September breach, written with the firm Merlon it hired to investigate. The attacker entered on September 21 through two zero-days in Zammad, the helpdesk software behind the group's CSIRT mailboxes, and after gaining root "used AI agents to continue the attack"; the indicators DIVD lists are "the sheer volume of attack activity and the number of artefacts generated in a relatively short period" and "the extensive comments and documentation in the attack scripts, including comments in which the attack agents appear to communicate with one another." The framework was ARTEX, the open-source agentic penetration-testing tool this brief first met on October 10 in a CrowdStrike write-up and again on November 18 in an FBI advisory. DIVD's own metrics, which it publishes because "Nerds love metrics": 15 minutes and 11 seconds to the first alert, 19 hours to a formal response, 27 hours 41 minutes to containment, part of that being travel time to assemble the team at a hackerspace. What left: titles of about 41,000 tickets, 4,121 personal email addresses, 34 phone numbers, and the Google Workspace directory. Both zero-days are now CVEs, fixed in Zammad 7.2 and 7.2.2. ([DIVD: We got hacked](https://www.divd.nl/hacked/); [DIVD CSIRT case](https://csirt.divd.nl/cases/DIVD-2026-00014/); [Anadolu](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/europe/dutch-ethical-hacking-group-hit-by-suspected-autonomous-ai-agents/4137745))

The same tool is the one reporting links to Tuesday's CIA story. IBTimes, citing Nextgov, says ARTEX "is believed to have been used" in the breach of the unclassified open-source intelligence system shared by the CIA and ODNI, while noting that "the precise method of intrusion and the identity of the attackers remain unclear." The FBI's IC3 advisory describing ARTEX as a weaponized LLM-driven platform was published November 13, eleven days before the breach became public; it said actors had used the tool since at least August and had in some cases gone "from initial targeting to privileged access in less than three hours." I found no CIA or ODNI statement; an ODNI official told CNN there was no effect on classified systems. ([IBTimes](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/artex-ai-tool-cia-odni-cyberattack-1834649); [Anadolu: FBI advisory, Nov 18](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/fbi-warns-hackers-using-ai-powered-tool-to-automate-cyberattacks-worldwide/4128312))

Two more disclosures this week share a shape: the entry point was GitHub, and the damage ran through the build system. 1Password said Wednesday that on November 19 it found an attacker had used an employee's corporate GitHub account "to access source code and push malicious code to backend services, which downloaded administrative data pertaining to our customers": names, sign-in emails, account identifiers, creation and last-sign-in times, public keys, Secure Remote Password parameters, and the tokens in some invitation and recovery emails. The attacker also copied private source and reached the CI/CD system. Vault contents and the keys protecting them were not touched, the company says, and it does not hold account passwords or secret keys; it has rotated credentials and lengthened the one-time codes in invitation and recovery emails from six digits to ten alphanumeric characters. It has not said how many customers or how the account was compromised. Defang, a deployment service, disclosed that on November 12 an attacker acting as an over-permissioned development GitHub App pushed a commit to one of its repositories and tagged it as a release; production trusted release tags, so the code ran in CI with production credentials, and the main database was copied. The top Hacker News comment on 1Password calls the pattern "the standard fact pattern for every SAAS breach." Also on the ledger: FIFA confirmed to The Athletic a "criminal cyber-attack" after a group claimed 12 gigabytes of files; and Hanno Böck wrote to oss-security that Ubuntu had gone nine days without a fix for the NoisyTCP kernel bug. ([1Password](https://1password.com/blog/update-on-nov-2026-security-incident); [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644817); [Defang](https://defang.io/blog/post/security-incident-november-2026/); [Anadolu: FIFA](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/sports/fifa-confirms-cyberattack-as-hacking-group-claims-theft-of-sensitive-data-report/4138680); [oss-security](https://www.openwall.com/lists/oss-security/2026/11/25/9))

## Postgres as One File

Jeff Lindsay's minipg is PostgreSQL as a single executable with no server to configure: a `.pgdb` directory holds the data, config, credentials and Postgres version, `minipg app.pgdb` opens a psql shell on it, and a server starts when a command needs one and stops when idle. The trick is that it embeds Docker Hub's `postgres:18-alpine` image and runs Alpine's own Postgres binaries on the host hypervisor through h9, a Zig library whose Plan 9 kernel answers their system calls, so there is no Linux kernel to boot, no VM image and no Docker. There is a browser demo that downloads 35 MB and runs the Linux build inside the image itself on a unix socket. I have not run it; the page carries its own "warning!" section and I would read that first. ([minipg](https://progrium.github.io/minipg/))
