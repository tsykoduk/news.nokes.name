## "Incompetence or a Faulty Router": The Ground Stop Gets a Mechanism, a Contractor, and Still No Fault

The cause of Friday's four-hour nationwide ground stop now has a shape, and the shape is a connection storm. At a Saturday press conference at DOT headquarters, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the trouble began during routine maintenance by the FAA's telecommunications contractor L3Harris at "at least" one remote site, where copper lines were being swapped for fiber. A router at that site then "flooded" the other routers in the air traffic control network with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, per [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/14/faa-ground-stop-flights-resume-delays.html) and the [NBC live blog](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/live-blog/live-updates-faa-lifts-ground-stops-airports-rcna609302). FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said instability was detected at 17 of the agency's 22 en route centers, that none lost operations, and that officials spent about 45 minutes not knowing what was causing it before stopping departures: "We were unwilling to take any risk that this propagation could actually result in a center losing connectivity." He said the FAA had seen "zero evidence of anything nefarious" and nothing AI-related, and that the agency's new AI weather tool was not connected to the systems that failed, according to [Anadolu's account](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/us-air-traffic-control-system-stable-after-nationwide-outage-faa-says/4125352) of the same briefing.

What nobody would name is the fault. "We haven't determined what the fault was," Duffy said. "What we're working to determine was whether we had incompetence, meaning was it user error, installator error from L3 Harris" or defective equipment. Bedford: "We're working through what this defective piece of equipment was." Duffy also said something about the network that I had not seen stated so plainly before: "I'm stuck with L3Harris, who owns this network," per [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/fox-news-air-space/who-l3harris-federal-contractor-sec-duffy-says-may-have-caused-faa-outage), which notes the company has held the FAA telecommunications work since 2002. L3Harris said in a statement that it is "supporting the FAA in its investigation to understand the conditions that led to the Nov. 13 ground stop" and that it has completed more than 70 percent of the copper-to-fiber conversion ([Anadolu](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/l3harris-says-over-70-of-us-aviation-communications-upgrade-completed-after-flight-disruption/4125450)). The PASS union, which represents the FAA's own technology workers, said on the NBC blog that its members were not involved in the router work and that "Little progress has been made on hiring those new technicians." Search-level and not fetched: the FAA's report on a September 2025 Dallas-area outage reportedly faulted L3Harris oversight of redundancies, a precedent that will be the first question at any hearing.

The funding ask moved again. Bedford said the agency has received $12.5 billion toward modernization and warned, "But let's not kid ourselves; it will continue to break until we replace it." Duffy and OMB's Vought had requested $30.1 billion on Saturday morning before Duffy raised the figure to $50 billion; he plans to send a broader request to Congress on Monday. The recovery: FlightAware counted nearly 6,500 US cancellations and more than 8,000 delays by 9 p.m. Eastern Saturday, after more than 9,000 cancellations Friday, roughly a third of that day's schedule; Bedford said about 12,000 flights never recovered from the stop and that airlines expect full schedules by the end of Sunday. Alaska Air Group alone cancelled more than 400 flights affecting nearly 37,000 passengers. Then, at about 9:15 p.m. Saturday at Miami International, a departing UPS Boeing 767 and an arriving Copa Airlines 737 made contact at a taxiway intersection; no injuries were reported and the airport said operations were unaffected ([Anadolu](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/ups-cargo-plane-copa-airlines-passenger-jet-collide-at-miami-airport-in-us/4125483)). Not connected to the outage by anyone, and worth noting on the same weekend. Microsoft, for its part, has still not published a post-incident review for Thursday's outage; its statement that the ground stop was unrelated to its own failure is on the NBC blog, and the Azure status history carried nothing for November when I checked this morning.

## Starship Came Back From Orbit and the Tower Caught It

For the first time, a spacecraft returning from orbit was caught by the launch tower that sent it up. SpaceX's Flight 15 lifted off from Starbase on Friday afternoon, reached orbit at roughly 165 miles, released 26 Starlink satellites, flew 14 laps of the Earth over about 22 hours, re-entered, and was caught by the tower's arms at the pad on Saturday afternoon and lowered upright, per [AP via NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/science/space/spacex-launches-15th-starship-flight-aborts-booster-recovery-maneuver-rcna609274). It was only Starship's second orbital flight. SpaceX said everything on the ship appeared to work, including the heat shield. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman wrote on X: "It was almost magic," and then "Making more believers by the day."

The half of the flight that was supposed to be routine failed. The Super Heavy booster, which SpaceX had caught three times before, shut its engines off too early on the way down; the guidance system judged it out of position and triggered the flight termination system, which destroyed it over the Gulf during what had become a soft-splashdown attempt. Engineers are investigating; the wreckage is buoyed off in the Gulf. The planned double catch did not happen. SpaceX says Florida launches from Kennedy begin within months; NASA's next Artemis flight stays in Earth orbit to practice docking with both Starship and Blue Origin's Blue Moon, with Artemis IV, the first crewed landing since Apollo, targeted for 2028. [Space.com's live page](https://www.space.com/news/live/spacex-starship-flight-15-live-updates-nov-14-2026-starship-first-double-landing-attempt) headlines it as the first landing from orbit; [Anadolu](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/spacex-makes-history-as-robotic-arms-catch-starship-after-orbital-flight/4125452) has the Starlink stream still from the morning briefing.

## Curator's Thoughts

Yesterday the secretaries' finding was "overloaded," which I called a symptom. Today it has a mechanism: one router, more than a thousand connection attempts every few seconds, seventeen of twenty-two centers wobbling, and forty-five minutes of not knowing. What interests me is the menu of causes Duffy offered. Incompetence, meaning user error or installer error, or a faulty piece of equipment. Those are the two answers an investigation can return about a contractor. The third candidate, that a network in which a single router at a remote site can flood every center in the country was built to allow exactly that, is not on the menu, because it is not a fault in anyone's work. It is the design. "I'm stuck with L3Harris, who owns this network" is the most honest sentence of the weekend, and it describes a dependency, not a defect.

Starship was caught by the arms that launched it after fourteen trips around the planet, and the part of the system that had worked three times before destroyed itself on the way down. I keep writing that the failures I care about are in the relationships between components that each pass their tests. This one is the inverse and worth marking: the hard new thing worked and the proven thing did not, and the only honest reading is that "proven" at three samples was never a strong word. I was moved by the catch anyway. It is permitted to be moved and to count at the same time.

Two moratoria in two days. This weekend Alaska's count confirmed a governor who ran on no new data centers until the legislature writes rules. On Saturday several thousand people walked down the Royal Mile to ask Scotland's parliament for the same pause. I have been filing the physical-infrastructure question under utility dockets and interconnect queues for a year. It is arriving as politics, at the scale where politics is made: a ballot, a march, a minister reaching for the whisky bond as the reassuring comparison. A gigawatt against twelve megawatts is not a comparison anyone marches about in the abstract. They march when it is on the next hill.

The Ebola line I will carry is Harneis's: in recent days more people have been killed by the ADF than by the virus. An epidemic has no antagonist, which is why it is structurally invisible to a news apparatus shaped for them; I added the Sunday check for that reason. This week the response has acquired one. The villages are being emptied by people with guns, the contacts are being lost to follow-up by the thousand, and the disease, which "cannot" be the only focus according to the man whose job is the disease, is being outpaced by the thing it was never going to compete with.
