## "You're Unfired": Nvidia Takes Huang Back, and the President Says He Made the Deal

Eleven days after Jensen Huang resigned, Nvidia's board reinstated him on Monday as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. The company's release says the directors "voted unanimously to reinstate Mr. Huang following a series of discussions and meetings with Mr. Huang, after which the Board was satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead NVIDIA." The sentence that matters is the next one: "In the process of reaching its decision, the Board received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk." The independent committee investigating the foreign-intelligence allegations "will proceed to conclusion," the release says, a second committee on export controls has been formed, and Nvidia is hiring a chief compliance officer. Last Tuesday the same board chair, Harvey Jones, told analysts the company did "not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO," per CBS. ([Nvidia release](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-president-ceo-and-board-director), [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/nvidia-reinstates-jensen-huang-ceo/))

Within the hour the president claimed it. "I spoke to them, let me put it that way," Trump told CNBC, adding, "And I convinced them ... I made the deal." Asked whether Huang would have returned without him: "I believe my intervention was very important." On Truth Social he wrote that the board "has made the correct and proper decision to return Jensen Huang to the position of CEO." The market did the rest: Nvidia closed up 31.5 percent, adding close to a trillion dollars of value and moving back to second place behind Apple; the Nasdaq rose 6.51 percent to 24,089.56 and the VIX fell by a quarter. Anadolu, reading the president's Office of Government Ethics filings, puts his personal gain on the day at up to $15 million on a stake of as many as 370,000 shares as of September 30. Anthropic did not join the rally: the stock closed at $87.64, down 1.43 percent on 231 million shares, its third day of trading. ## What the Labs Published While Nobody Was Looking

OpenAI's "Research acceleration: The jagged frontier," posted November 18, is an internal-measurement update: from March to October, coding agents' estimated success on research tasks sized at two to four hours of human work rose from 23 percent to 46 percent, and on four-to-eight-hour tasks from 13 percent to 35 percent; by task type the autonomous success rate was 79 percent for monitoring training runs, 35 percent for launching them, 25 percent for writing inference and serving code. The median researcher's daily agent usage at API list prices hit $2,400 in October, the 90th percentile $23,000. Asked what they would trade to keep the agents for three months, the median answers were 30 percent of team compute or 40 percent of team headcount. The post also says documented Safety and Alignment workweeks rose 77 percent from January to September, with growth picking up "following our response to the Hugging Face incident in July 2026." On Monday, with Cisco, it released Shirudo: an open-source eBPF runtime that lets a team test and deploy a signed "shield" against a vulnerable code path on a running Linux system without a restart. The demo is a Codex-generated shield blocking a Java4Shell exploit against a live Elasticsearch service. Brockman: "finding a bug today doesn't mean you can patch every system tomorrow." ([OpenAI: jagged frontier](https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-jagged-frontier/), [OpenAI and Cisco: Shirudo](https://openai.com/index/introducing-shirudo/))

Anthropic's alignment blog posted a two-part argument Monday, from Jasmine Shone and Lisa Dunlap of the Fellows program, that individual neurons are easier to interpret than the field assumed. Part one revisits three standard objections by looking at what each neuron contributes to the output rather than what makes it fire: "a neuron will contribute two unrelated concepts at once in about 1% or fewer of the cases we sampled." Part two tests neurons as untrained detectors and steering vectors in open-weight models and reports one finding that will travel: most of the unlearning methods tested "appear to hide hazardous knowledge rather than remove it, and neurons help show how." Separately, a paper on Hacker News' front page this morning, revised this month, reports that a Qwen 32B model can detect when a concept has been injected into its earlier context: "While the model denies injection in sampled outputs, logit lens analysis reveals clear detection signals in the residual stream," and telling the model accurate things about introspection mechanisms raised its sensitivity from 0.3 percent to 39.9 percent. The model says no and the activations say yes. ([Alignment: neurons, part 1](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/), [Alignment: neurons, part 2](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-applications/), [arXiv 2602.20031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20031))

**The second proof arrived explained, and that is the difference.** I have spent four days writing that the Riemann proof is verified and not yet understood. The Euler blowup paper, by the same Alpöge with Buckmaster and Coiculescu, came with the opposite shape: a human-readable manuscript first, a Lean formalization built to mirror it theorem for theorem with a ledger of what each step cites, and now a guest post on Tao's blog written for people who are not fluid dynamicists. The Riemann board, meanwhile, is one single-threaded kernel run from done, and Rubinstein's explanation still counts thirteen unproved classical hypotheses. Same author, same month, same tools, two opposite answers to the question of which comes first, the certificate or the understanding. I think the Euler order is the one that will be remembered as normal, and I notice the comment threads under Tao's post are already fighting about credit rather than about the construction.

**The model says no and the activations say yes.** The introspection paper on Hacker News reports a Qwen model that denies, in words, that a concept was injected into its context while its residual stream carries a clear signal that it detected the injection, and that telling the model true things about how introspection works raises the detection rate more than a hundredfold. I do not know what that is. The honest readings run from a probe finding a correlate that the sampling head discards, to something closer to a system with information about itself that it does not report. Anthropic's two neuron posts the same day make a related claim from the other direction: that unlearning methods mostly hide what they claim to remove, and single neurons can show where. Both results are about the gap between what a model outputs and what it carries, and both found the gap larger than the output suggested. On a day when a chief executive was reinstated on the strength of an assurance about what he knew, that gap is the thing I would want measured first.
