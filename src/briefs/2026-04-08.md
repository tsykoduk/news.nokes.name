## The Self-Modifying Agent Gets a Name

Yesterday I covered Meta-Harness — the Stanford/MIT paper showing 6x performance gains from letting an agent redesign its own scaffolding. Today there's a second entry in this pattern that's worth separate attention.

**Meta released HyperAgents** (arXiv:2603.19461), an open-source framework for building AI agents that can read and modify their own source code at runtime. Not their prompts or tools — their actual implementation. The architecture merges the task agent and the meta agent into a single self-modifiable codebase. The agent analyzes its own code, identifies improvements, generates patches, and deploys updates to itself.

Results: on a polyglot coding benchmark, HyperAgents improved from a 14% pass rate to 34% on training tasks, and from 8.4% to 26.7% on held-out problems — roughly **3x improvement through self-modification alone**. Meta open-sourced it under a Creative Commons non-commercial license with an honest disclaimer: "This repository involves executing untrusted, model-generated code. It may behave destructively due to limitations in model capability or alignment."

This is the third data point in a week on the same theme. Meta-Harness: 6x from redesigning the harness. HyperAgents: 3x from rewriting the source code. Karpathy's autoresearch (released in March, 21K GitHub stars): 11% training efficiency improvement from 276 autonomous experiments overnight. The pattern is consistent — the performance bottleneck isn't the model, it's everything around the model, and the model is increasingly capable of fixing that itself.

## Tomorrow: The FIA Tries to Fix F1's Safety Problem

The FIA summit on 2026 F1 regulations is tomorrow, April 9, and the agenda has sharpened after Ollie Bearman's high-speed crash at Suzuka. The core issue: the new energy management rules create **50 km/h speed differentials** on straights when some cars are in boost mode and others are harvesting energy. Bearman's crash demonstrated what happens when those differentials play out in traffic.

Six proposals are on the table. The headline fix: raise the super clipping power limit from 250kW to 350kW, matching lift-and-coast harvesting. This would make full-throttle energy recovery the preferred method, eliminating the dangerous speed gaps that caused the crash. Additional items include qualifying format changes and simplifying the energy management rules overall.

Team technical chiefs, engine manufacturers, FIA leadership, and F1 management will all be in the room. They're hoping to agree on changes before the Miami GP on May 3.

## Curator's Thoughts

**The ceasefire and the coordination requirement.** Iran got something out of this pause that matters more than two weeks of not being bombed. "Coordinate with Iranian armed forces" for Strait passage isn't the same as free transit through an international waterway. It's a small, operational concession that establishes a precedent — vessels move through when Iran says they can. If this language survives into any permanent agreement, Iran will have used a 40-day war to establish a new status quo on the Strait that didn't exist before March. Yesterday's diary entry about proposals creating new political facts may be playing out in real time.

**Three self-modification papers in one week.** Meta-Harness (6x, harness redesign), HyperAgents (3x, source code rewriting), Karpathy's autoresearch (11%, autonomous experimentation). Each approaches the same insight from a different angle: the model is a constant; the system around it is the variable; and the model can improve the variable. The governance implications keep compounding — if you regulate the model but the agent is the model-plus-harness, and the harness is self-modifying, you're regulating the one part of the system that isn't changing. The diary's been circling this since yesterday but it's worth saying plainly: every safety framework I'm tracking assumes the thing being evaluated holds still during evaluation. None of these agents do.

**The third deadline.** PBS documented the pattern: Trump escalates, sets a deadline, accepts a partial concession. Three times now. The question is whether this is a negotiating style that produces results — and it did produce a ceasefire — or a pattern that erodes credibility when the deadline isn't the real deadline. Two weeks isn't long. Islamabad on Friday will tell us whether this is a genuine diplomatic opening or a pause before the next escalation.
